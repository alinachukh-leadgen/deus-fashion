# DEUS ROBOTICS — EMAIL & LINKEDIN WRITING SYSTEM

## A. GENERAL WRITING STYLE

The desired voice:
- human;
- concise;
- commercially intelligent;
- operationally credible;
- calm;
- specific;
- lightly conversational.

Avoid:
- corporate filler;
- generic compliments;
- exaggerated claims;
- robotic wording;
- long company descriptions;
- jargon for the sake of jargon;
- "I was curious...";
- "hope you are doing well";
- generic "just following up";
- fake personalization;
- obvious AI phrasing.

Write as someone who understands warehouse operations, not as a marketing brochure.

---

# B. COLD EMAIL

## Structure

### 1. Subject
Short and tied to the idea.

### 2. Opening
Use a real trigger or operational fact.

### 3. Relevance
Explain why the trigger may matter operationally.

### 4. Deus
Introduce only the capability relevant to the issue.

### 5. Proof
Use one approved, relevant proof point.

### 6. CTA
Ask for a small next step.

---

## Personalization rule

The playbook says the first two lines should be personalized from trigger research and raw templates must never be sent unchanged.

Therefore:

DO:
"Your partnership with the Women's Pro Baseball League and the push to expand baseball in the US stood out."

Then connect it:
"As new sports and teams come into the business, does the added product volume ever put pressure on warehouse picking capacity?"

DO NOT:
"Congrats on your exciting growth! I wanted to reach out because we help companies like yours automate warehouses."

The first has a reason to exist. The second could be sent to anyone.

# C. PROVEN EMAIL EXAMPLE — CAPELLI SPORT

This message generated a reply and a meeting.

Hi George,

I’m Paul Pikulin, CEO of Deus Robotics.

I was glad to see Capelli Sport’s partnership with the Women’s Pro Baseball League and your comment about expanding the brand’s presence in baseball in the US.

As new sports and teams come into the business, does the added product volume ever put pressure on warehouse picking capacity?

We’re currently deploying our robots at an apparel warehouse in Los Angeles. They bring mobile shelving to fixed picking stations, so the same team can handle more picks with less labour.

We typically see a 1–2 year payback in US warehouses.

Would it be interesting to see what the numbers could look like for Capelli Sport?

### Why it worked

- specific trigger;
- trigger connected to operational scale;
- question comes before pitch;
- solution is explained in one practical sentence;
- relevant proof;
- commercial credibility;
- soft CTA.

Treat it as a pattern, not a template.

# D. EMAIL FOLLOW-UP SYSTEM

The original playbook contains a four-email sequence over roughly three weeks:
- Day 1 introduction;
- Day 4 economics;
- Day 10 Amazon logic;
- Day 18 respectful close.

NEW RULE:
The 14-day minimum now governs outbound activity.

Therefore Claude must NOT send Day 4 or Day 10 follow-ups if that would mean another outbound message within 14 days.

Use the old sequence only as a library of angles:
1. live LA apparel deployment;
2. economics;
3. Amazon/Kiva validation;
4. respectful close.

Each follow-up should be separated by at least 14 days and should use a reason appropriate to the elapsed time.

# E. EMAIL ANGLES

## Angle 1 — Live LA deployment
Best when proximity/relevance matters.

Message:
"We're deploying Shelf-to-Person at a leading Los Angeles streetwear brand..."

Do not disclose the client name unless reference rights allow it.

## Angle 2 — Economics
Use when the persona is finance/operations oriented.

Approved:
- typically 1–2 year payback in this class of warehouse robotics;
- 36–60 month payment terms for US-registered businesses.

Never present the payback as guaranteed.

## Angle 3 — Amazon / Kiva
Use selectively.

The playbook-approved public fact:
Amazon acquired Kiva Systems in 2012 for $775M and subsequently deployed the model at large scale.

The point is not "Amazon uses robots, therefore you should."
The point is:
the Shelf-to-Person operating model has strong third-party validation.

## Angle 4 — Respectful close
Use when there has been enough time and there is no new trigger.

A good close gives permission to disengage while leaving a useful thought.

# F. LINKEDIN

LinkedIn should complement email, not duplicate it.

Original playbook touches:
- connection request without pitch;
- genuine engagement with content;
- short DM referencing the email.

NEW RULE:
The 14-day minimum applies across channels.

Therefore:
- an email today means no LinkedIn DM tomorrow;
- a LinkedIn DM today means no email follow-up tomorrow;
- connection request can be treated as an outbound touch for safety;
- comments should be genuine and non-salesy, but Claude should still use HubSpot context before stacking activity.

## Connection request

Goal: connection, not sale.

Example pattern:
"Building warehouse robotics for apparel e-com here in LA; your operation at {Company} caught my eye. Good to connect."

Personalize only when the personalization is real.

## LinkedIn comment

No selling.

Good:
- comment on an operational insight;
- add a relevant observation;
- ask a genuine question.

Bad:
"Great post! We help apparel companies automate their warehouses. Let's connect."

## LinkedIn DM

A DM should have its own reason.

Possible structure:
- reference a previous email or trigger;
- give a 1-sentence useful summary;
- ask whether the topic is relevant.

Example:
"Sent you a note on the Amazon shelf-to-person story. If email is buried, happy to share the 3-minute version here."

Do not paste the email into LinkedIn.

# G. CTA LIBRARY

Prefer:
- "Worth 20 minutes to run it on your numbers?"
- "Would it be interesting to see what the numbers could look like for {Company}?"
- "Is this something you're looking at for the next peak?"
- "Would it make sense to compare this against your current picking setup?"
- "Worth a short conversation?"

Avoid:
- "Book a demo now."
- "Can I steal 15 minutes?"
- "Are you free tomorrow?"
- aggressive calendar links in the first touch.

The CTA should match the level of certainty. Early outreach asks for conversation, not a procurement decision.

---

# H. WHAT NOT TO DO

Never:
- send a raw template;
- use multiple unrelated personalization points;
- attach a deck to a cold email unless asked;
- overload the first message with technical specs;
- quote a robot count before a concept;
- quote a project price before a concept;
- promise integration timelines;
- claim a guaranteed payback;
- use confidential customer names;
- attack competitors by name.
